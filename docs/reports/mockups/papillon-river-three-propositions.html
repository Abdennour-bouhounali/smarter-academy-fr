« Thalès dans le papillon » — the first dock, rebuilt

Three propositions for the step you refused (fixing the point R, then finding the distance AF). Nothing is coded. Choose one, mix them, or refuse all three.

What was wrong with the old step. The student walked inland until « le piquet S cache le poteau F dans l'œil de Zéro ». That alignment cannot be seen on the screen: open water and one small letter. Then he typed AF into an empty box, with no figure of what he was measuring. The butterfly, the whole point of the lesson, never appeared before his eyes.
The rule all three propositions follow: the two triangles are always visible, the gesture moves something he can see, and the unknown length is drawn before it is asked.
Proposition 1 · a new situation

Le chadouf — the lever that lifts the water

The situation
On the bank, a chadouf: a beam pivoting on a post O, a short arm with the counterweight, a long arm with the bucket. The machine IS a butterfly: two triangles opposite at the pivot.
What he does
He pulls the short arm down by a number of rope knots. Before letting go he says how high the bucket rises, by placing a mark on the bucket's pole. The beam turns: the bucket stops at the mark, under it or over it.
What he sees
The two triangles shaded on either side of the pivot from the first second, their sides labelled as he measures them.
Why it teaches
« Chaque longueur part du sommet » is physical here: the arm lengths are measured from the pivot. The 4e habit (taking the whole beam) gives a visibly wrong height.
Cost
A new machine to model in 3D at the dock, and the forge's first round rewritten on it. The largest of the three.
Proposition 2 · the river kept, seen from above

Le plan du géomètre — the strings stretched on the plan

The situation
The same river and the same question (how long is the ferry rope?), but played on the builders' plan, seen from above, the whole time.
What he does
A red string is tied to the far post F and passes over the stake S. He slides R along the dotted line from E: the string follows his hand, and R is right when the string is straight, which he sees. Then the plan draws the rope AF as a dotted line and he sets its length on a ruler laid beside it; the ferry rope is thrown: it ties, falls short or hangs slack.
What he sees
Both triangles shaded as soon as R is placed; every measured length written on its side.
Why it teaches
Same mathematics as today, but the alignment is a string, not an eye, and the unknown has a picture.
Cost
The smallest: the dock's 3D stays as scenery; the step becomes a 2D plan (the world already has a top view).
Proposition 3 · a new situation

Le tréteau des peintres — the trestle that opens

The situation
The painters of the temple work on a plank laid on an X trestle: two poles pinned together at O. The plank must fit the tops exactly.
What he does
He slides the feet apart or together along the ground. Before the plank is laid he chooses its length among the planks leaning on the wall; the painters lay it: it fits, it falls through, or it sticks out.
What he sees
The X in front of him: the triangle of the feet below the pin, the triangle of the tops above, moving together as he slides.
Why it teaches
The pin is the vertex, and each length runs from the pin along a pole. The floor and the plank are parallel because the trestle stands level. It also prepares lesson 3 (is the plank level?).
Cost
Medium: one new object at the dock and its sliding gesture (the world's one gesture), the forge's first round rewritten on it.
